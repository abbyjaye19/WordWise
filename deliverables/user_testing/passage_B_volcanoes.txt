How Volcanoes Form

Deep under our feet, the Earth is extremely hot. In some places it is hot enough to melt rock. This melted rock is called magma. Because magma is lighter than the solid rock around it, it slowly rises toward the surface.

The outer layer of the Earth is broken into giant pieces called tectonic plates. These plates move very slowly, only a few centimeters each year. Many volcanoes form where two plates push together or pull apart, because those places let magma escape more easily.

As magma rises, it collects in a large pool under the ground called a magma chamber. Gases trapped inside the magma build up pressure, a little like the bubbles in a shaken bottle of soda. When the pressure becomes too great, the magma bursts through cracks in the ground. This is an eruption.

Once magma reaches the surface, it is called lava. When lava cools, it hardens into new rock. Over many eruptions, layers of this rock pile up and form the cone shape of a volcano.

Some volcanoes erupt gently, with lava flowing slowly. Others erupt with powerful explosions that throw ash high into the sky.
